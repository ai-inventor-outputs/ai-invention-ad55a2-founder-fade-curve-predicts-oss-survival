# Temporal Methods for OSS Survival Prediction

## Summary

This research investigated temporal methods used for predicting Open Source Software (OSS) project survival outcomes. The investigation focused on three main categories: survival analysis with time-varying covariates, deep learning sequence models (LSTM/GRU/Transformer), and workflow/dynamics analysis. The key finding is that while existing temporal methods primarily use aggregate project-level metrics or focus on process-level workflow patterns, no prior work specifically models founder-specific involvement trajectories or applies scaffolding/fading theory to understand founder departure impacts. Our founder fade curve approach advances the field by isolating founder behavior, extracting interpretable shape descriptors, and implementing falsification controls to test founder-specific effects.

## Research Findings

Temporal methods for OSS survival prediction primarily fall into three categories: (1) survival analysis with time-varying covariates (typically using project-level aggregates like commit frequency), (2) deep learning sequence models (LSTM/GRU/Transformer) modeling aggregate activity patterns, and (3) workflow/dynamics analysis focusing on pull request review patterns. Our founder fade curve approach advances these methods by: (a) isolating founder-specific involvement trajectories rather than aggregate project metrics, (b) extracting interpretable shape descriptors (slope, convexity) from involvement curves rather than using black-box sequence models, and (c) explicitly testing whether founder trajectories predict survival better than non-founder trajectories through a falsification control. While Karim et al. (2026) model 24-month temporal sequences using Transformer architectures, they use aggregate repository activity without isolating individual contributors. Kaushik & Chahal (2026) analyze PR workflow dynamics but focus on process-level metrics rather than founder behavior. No prior work applies scaffolding/fading theory to model founder departure in OSS, representing a significant theoretical gap our approach fills.[1][2][3]

Identify a 'death spiral' pattern characterized by declining innovation rates, exponential backlog growth, and rising merge latency. Focuses on workflow dynamics and PR-level metrics rather than founder-specific behavior or temporal involvement patterns.

Represents static/snapshot analysis rather than temporal modeling, but highlights importance of governance structures in project survival.
